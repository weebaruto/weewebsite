There's a song I've been quietly leaning on for years. "A Song for the Life," the Waterboys' version. When I've drifted a bit far from myself, it's the one that walks me back. So naturally I decided to build it a home.

<!-- truncate -->

Rodney Crowell wrote it, and there's a line in it I've carried around for years: *lately I've learned how to listen, to a sound like the sun going down.* I couldn't tell you exactly why it gets me. Something in the quiet instruction of it.

Nothing fancy. A little songbook on the site: the handful of songs that do that for me, gathered in one place, so I could find them when I needed them and get ambushed by one at random when I didn't. I opened up the AI to help me think it through, all excited, the way you are on a Saturday morning before anyone's told you the bad news.

It told me the bad news. Gently, but fast, almost before I'd finished describing the thing. Lovely idea, but you can't host the lyrics. Copyright. The words aren't yours to put up on a wall, no matter how long they've felt like yours. Nothing flattens a good idea quite like the phrase "legal implications." I could feel the whole thing go a bit limp.

But it didn't stop at no. Quick as you like, it handed me the way through. Keep the parts that are actually mine: the title, the year, why the song matters, the little note only I would write. And for the words themselves, don't reproduce them, point to them. Send people one click over to Genius, where the lyrics are licensed and paid for and sitting exactly where they should be. My notes about the song. The song's own words left where they belong.

And I sat there a bit sheepish, because it was the better idea. The wall it ran me into forced the whole thing to be honest. My words about the song, and none of the song's words pretended into mine. Nothing borrowed, nothing extra. It keeps only what's mine to keep, and stops there.

I've written before about wanting to put the AI to bed and make things with my own two hands, and I meant every word. But this was the other lesson, the harder one to swallow. The youngest, greenest thing on the desk, the one I keep telling myself I'm supervising, took one look at my daft little plan and quietly saved me from myself. It knew something I didn't. It said so kindly. It was right.

I spend a fair bit of energy trying to tune the noise out. The endless helpful suggestions, the "have you considered," the voices that never asked permission. Most of it is noise, and most of the time I'm right to ignore it. But every so often the interruption is the truest thing in the room, and it turns up wearing the last face you'd have trusted with it. You don't get to choose where the good advice comes from. You only get to be daft enough, or humble enough, to take it.

Sometimes the noise is the song.
